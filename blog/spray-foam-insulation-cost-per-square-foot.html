Spray Foam Insulation Cost per Square Foot: What Drives the Price

Typical Ranges, the Variables That Move Them, and How to Read a Quote

By Harvey Barnes July 21, 2026 6 min read

Pricing per Square Foot, but Lots of Variables Behind It

Spray foam is priced per square foot — but what actually drives the number up or down. Two homeowners asking "how much does spray foam cost" can easily get quotes that are 50 percent apart for the same project, because the per-square-foot price is a function of the job, not just the foam.

Here is a practical breakdown of the typical price ranges, the factors that move them, and how to read a spray foam quote without surprises. Treat the numbers below as benchmarks from industry cost guides, not as guaranteed quotes from any specific contractor.

Typical Price Ranges

Most residential spray foam jobs in the U.S. fall into a fairly predictable range.

  • Open-cell spray foam: roughly $1 to $3 per square foot installed in new construction, and closer to $3 to $5 per square foot in retrofit projects where access is limited.
  • Closed-cell spray foam: roughly $2 to $5 per square foot installed, with thicker assemblies, structural applications, and below-grade work pushing higher.

Those numbers come from industry cost guides and reflect the most common U.S. residential work. Your market may sit higher or lower — the Tri-State region and Northeast tend to run above the national average because of labor, fuel, and material costs. For a closer look at how these numbers compare, see the spray foam vs fiberglass comparison.

Open Cell vs. Closed Cell Pricing

The two products have different price profiles for structural reasons. Open-cell foam is lighter, uses less raw chemical per board foot, and is generally the cheaper product per board foot. Closed-cell foam is denser, uses more chemical, and achieves a higher R-value per inch — which is why its per-square-foot installed price is higher.

The decision is rarely about cost alone. Closed-cell may need less thickness to hit the code-required R-value, which can make the total job cost closer than the per-square-foot number suggests. The right call depends on the assembly, the moisture profile, and the performance goal.

New Construction vs. Retrofit

New construction is the cheaper category. The framing is open, the surfaces are accessible, and there is no drywall, insulation, or finish to work around. A crew can hit a target thickness quickly with minimal prep.

Retrofit costs more. Removing or working around existing drywall, cleaning the substrate, masking off finished spaces, and managing occupant access all add labor. The same foam that costs $1 to $3 per square foot in a new attic often costs $3 to $5 per square foot when sprayed into a finished wall cavity.

Job Size

Small jobs have a higher per-square-foot cost. The reason is overhead: every job requires mobilization, equipment setup, hose runs, daily safety setup, and cleanup. On a 200-square-foot rim joist job, that overhead is spread over very little footage. On a 4,000-square-foot attic, it almost disappears.

Most contractors have a minimum charge for the same reason — a half-day of setup and travel is the same whether you are spraying 200 or 2,000 square feet. If your job is small, expect the per-square-foot number to look high, and ask the contractor how they handle minimums.

Geographic Region

Spray foam pricing varies meaningfully by region. The Northeast — including the Tri-State area of New York, Connecticut, and New Jersey — sits on the higher end of the national range because of labor costs, fuel, building code requirements, and the cost of living. The South and parts of the Midwest typically run lower.

Material costs are national, but labor, fuel, and overhead are local. A quote in Brewster, NY, will not match a quote in Dallas, TX, and both can be honest numbers for their markets.

Thickness Required

Code-required R-value drives how many inches of foam the contractor sprays, which drives material cost. An attic in climate zone 5 may need R-49 to R-60, which translates to 12 to 17 inches of open-cell or 7 to 10 inches of closed-cell. A wall assembly may need R-13 to R-21, which is 3 to 6 inches of either product.

More inches means more chemical, more spray time, and a higher per-square-foot material cost — though the per-square-foot installed cost often does not change much, because the labor is similar.

Key point: When comparing quotes, make sure each one is based on the same R-value target. A $2-per-square-foot closed-cell quote at R-21 is not the same job as a $2-per-square-foot open-cell quote at R-13, even though the per-foot number looks identical.

Accessibility

Accessibility is one of the largest swing factors in a per-square-foot price. An open attic with a clear path for hose runs is the cheapest assembly to spray. A crawl space with limited entry, a rim joist that requires lifts or scaffolding, or a cathedral ceiling that needs hose extensions all push the per-square-foot labor cost up.

Spray foam crews are paid by the day, and a difficult access job covers less square footage in that day than an easy one. The math flows directly into the per-square-foot price.

Prep Work

Prep work is the part of the job the homeowner usually doesn't see, and it shows up in the price in three ways:

  • Protecting windows, fixtures, and finishes. Overspray on a window is expensive to fix, so masking is mandatory on most jobs.
  • Protecting occupants. Occupied homes require containment, ventilation, and re-entry times per the spray foam manufacturer's instructions.
  • Cleaning the substrate. Oil, dust, and loose material on the surface can prevent proper adhesion, and the contractor has to clean or treat the surface before spraying.

Each of these adds labor, and labor is what moves the per-square-foot price the most.

What's Included in the Quote

Two quotes for "the same job" can differ by thousands of dollars for legitimate reasons. The line items that show up in a thorough quote include:

  • Materials — the foam chemicals, primer, and any ignition or thermal barrier required by code.
  • Labor — typically a two-person crew for the duration of the job.
  • Setup and breakdown — equipment mobilization, daily safety setup, and teardown.
  • Prep and protection — masking, surface prep, and post-job cleanup.
  • Warranty — material and workmanship coverage, and what it actually covers.

Cheaper quotes sometimes skip the ignition barrier or thin on labor. Always ask what is included before comparing numbers.

How to Read a Spray Foam Quote

A clear quote should tell you four things: the foam type, the target R-value, the total board feet or square footage at each thickness, and what is included in the price. If a quote only shows a single per-square-foot number, ask for the breakdown.

Questions worth asking before signing:

  • What R-value is this quote designed to deliver?
  • What is the installed thickness, in inches, in each area?
  • Is the ignition or thermal barrier included?
  • What warranty covers the material and the labor?
  • Is there a minimum charge, and what does it cover?

A contractor who can answer those clearly is usually a contractor who can deliver a clean job. Plan for a price range, not a single number, and you'll have a much better conversation with whoever shows up to bid.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick Answers

How much does spray foam cost per square foot installed?

Industry cost guides put open-cell spray foam at roughly $1 to $3 per square foot installed in new construction and $3 to $5 in retrofit, while closed-cell runs about $2 to $5 per square foot installed, depending on thickness, access, and region. Treat these as benchmarks, not quotes.

Why is closed-cell spray foam more expensive?

Closed-cell foam uses more raw chemical per board foot, achieves a higher R-value per inch, and acts as a vapor barrier — all of which raise the per-square-foot price. It is often the right call when a thinner assembly, structural rigidity, or moisture control is required.

Is spray foam worth the cost vs fiberglass?

In most attics, rim joists, and basements, yes. The air sealing, moisture control, and longevity typically offset the higher upfront cost through lower energy bills and fewer callbacks. For interior walls in conditioned space, fiberglass is often the smarter spend.

Can I get a discount for a larger spray foam job?

Usually, yes. Per-square-foot pricing drops as the job grows because mobilization, setup, and overhead are spread over more footage. Ask the contractor for a per-thousand-board-foot or per-square-foot price break once the job crosses a few thousand square feet.

What is the cheapest way to insulate with spray foam?

The cheapest spray foam job is an open-cell installation on an open, accessible surface at code-minimum thickness in new construction. That combination minimizes material and labor. Anything that adds prep work, access difficulty, or thickness will push the price up.
