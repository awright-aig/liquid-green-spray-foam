Why Pennsylvania Is a Strong Franchise Market

Pennsylvania is the fifth-largest state in the country by population, with approximately 13 million residents spread across distinct regional markets. Philadelphia and the southeastern counties anchor the east. The Lehigh Valley (Allentown, Bethlehem, Easton) sits in the east-central corridor and is one of the fastest-growing metros in the state. Harrisburg and the Susquehanna Valley run up the center. Scranton and the Wyoming Valley cover the northeast. Pittsburgh and the southwest have their own self-contained economy. State College and the central PA counties tie the I-80 corridor, and Erie anchors the northwest on Lake Erie.

Geographically, the state spans three or more IECC climate zones — warmer along the southeastern border, colder in the Poconos and the northwest, and high-elevation cold in the Allegheny Plateau. That diversity is a feature, not a bug. It means four-season demand in most markets, with the colder zones producing the strongest heating-degree-day economics for insulation work. The state has a four-season install window in most regions, and a robust builder base that runs year-round.

The other Pennsylvania feature is the housing stock. The median age of owner-occupied homes in the state is around 60 years, per the most recent U.S. census data. That means a large share of homes were built before modern energy codes — pre-1970s construction with little or no insulation, single-pane windows, and significant air leakage. Each of those homes is a candidate for a deep energy retrofit, and the older the home, the bigger the energy savings from a high-performance spray foam install. The retrofit pipeline is decades long.

Construction Industry & New Build

Pennsylvania's construction market is one of the largest and most diverse in the country. Statewide construction spending runs in the tens of billions annually, with continuous activity across residential, commercial, institutional, and infrastructure categories. Philadelphia alone runs a deep pipeline of residential, mixed-use, and institutional work, with major universities (Temple, Drexel, Penn), healthcare systems, and a steady stream of public projects. The Lehigh Valley is one of the fastest-growing metros in the state, with new construction in residential and industrial categories.

Pittsburgh has its own construction economy, anchored by the energy and healthcare sectors. UPMC, Allegheny Health Network, and the universities (Pitt, Carnegie Mellon, Duquesne) drive a steady institutional pipeline. The southwest also has the rust-belt retrofit market — older housing stock, post-industrial buildings, and a strong demand for energy efficiency upgrades. Scranton and the Wyoming Valley have a similar retrofit profile, with older homes and steady builder activity.

Institutional construction is a major slice of PA demand. The state-related university system — Penn State, the University of Pittsburgh, Temple, and Lincoln — generates continuous capital projects. K-12 construction, healthcare capital projects, and state agency infrastructure add to the pipeline. For a spray foam operator, this is high-value commercial work that compounds the residential base. A statewide development develops sub-territories for each major metro and builds a regional platform with both residential and commercial work in the mix.

Energy Efficiency Tailwind

Pennsylvania runs a layered set of energy efficiency programs that put real money into the hands of homeowners and building owners. The statewide utility rebate programs — administered by the major Pennsylvania utilities including PPL, Met-Ed (FirstEnergy), PECO, Duquesne Light, and Penelec — pay for insulation and air-sealing work in residential and commercial properties. Each utility has its own portfolio and incentive structure, but the pattern is consistent: a real dollar amount per project, paid to the contractor or the homeowner, for qualifying work performed by a participating contractor.

On the income-eligible side, the Pennsylvania weatherization program (LIHEAP and the state weatherization assistance program) funds free insulation, air sealing, and HVAC upgrades for households below a defined income threshold. Approved contractors get a steady stream of paying work without chasing the bid. The combination of utility rebates, weatherization funding, and federal tax credits stacks the financing for the homeowner in a way that closes more bids at full price.

On the federal side, the Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit (Section 25C) is the main lever. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed 2025) extended Section 25C through 2032 with an annual $3,200 cap and a $1,200 sub-limit for envelope work. Codes do the same work from the new construction side: Pennsylvania's statewide building code is based on the 2018 or 2021 IECC, with R-value and air-sealing requirements that point at spray foam as a high-performance solution. The structural tailwind is real and consistent across the state.

Local Work & Jobs Across the State

A statewide Pennsylvania development creates local jobs in every sub-territory, with the same playbook applied across regions. The work is spray foam installed in homes, schools, commercial buildings, agricultural structures, and retrofits. The crews are local. The hires are local. The customers are local. Operators hire in their own communities, train through the Liquid Green Spray Academy, and serve neighbors.

Job creation scales with the development. A single territory operator hires spray foam technicians, a crew lead, an estimator, and office support as the business grows. A statewide operator adds regional managers, recruiting capacity, and sub-franchisee support roles. Each new sub-territory brings its own local hiring. The roles are skilled trades positions, full-time and year-round, with on-the-job training through the Spray Academy. The pay is above the local average for entry-level work.

Pennsylvania has a strong work ethic and trades culture. The people who come into this business want a career, not a side job. The Spray Academy and the HQ onboarding give them the technical and operational foundation; the operator gives them the team and the customer pipeline. The result is a stable, growing local business that hires from the community, trains in the community, and serves the community. That is the work opportunity the franchise model is built on.
