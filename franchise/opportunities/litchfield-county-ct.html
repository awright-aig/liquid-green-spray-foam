Why Litchfield County, CT Is a Strong Franchise Market

Litchfield County is unusual. It is the largest county in Connecticut by land area — roughly 920 square miles of rolling hills, stone walls, forests, and farmland — and at the same time the smallest by population, with approximately 185,000 residents. That combination is exactly what a spray foam operator wants: fewer neighbors, but wealthier neighbors, on bigger lots, who own older houses that are expensive to heat and cool.

Per the most recent U.S. Census data, Litchfield County has one of the highest median home values in the state — well above $400,000 in towns like Washington, Roxbury, Sharon, and Kent. Properties commonly sit on 5, 10, even 20+ acre parcels, so jobs are larger in square footage than the typical suburban install. The work is concentrated in fewer but higher-value projects, and pricing follows the housing stock.

The customer base is unusually strong. Litchfield County has a deep agricultural and equestrian economy — horse farms, dairy operations, hay barns, riding arenas, and outbuildings that all need insulation and air sealing — alongside healthcare, education, and a deep layer of second-home owners from New York City. The work runs year-round, with very little of the cyclicality you see in purely commuter-belt markets. For a first-time operator, that is a real foundation.

Construction Industry & New Build in the Litchfield Hills

Connecticut's residential construction market continues to run above the long-term average in dollar terms. In Litchfield County, new construction skews toward custom single-family homes on large lots — the kind of build where the developer and GC are deeply involved in product decisions and where a high-R-value insulation package is sold as a feature, not an upgrade.

Spray foam is well-suited to the Litchfield build. Cathedral ceilings, timber-frame additions, post-and-beam barn conversions, and high-performance net-zero designs are common, and builders in Washington, Cornwall, and Salisbury have come to expect closed-cell foam on rooflines and rim joists and open-cell on attic floors. The bid process is relationship-driven, not commodity.

For a new franchise operator, that creates a real runway. Builders and GCs need a reliable trade partner who shows up on time, hits the spec, and documents the install. The Liquid Green playbook is to win those early jobs, get photos and referrals, and turn them into the next ten. Add in the renovation and addition market — and Litchfield has a deep one, with 18th- and 19th-century colonials being updated for modern efficiency — and the construction opportunity is durable. New builds fuel one side of the business, retrofits fuel the other. Both are local work for local crews.

Energy Efficiency Tailwind in Northwest Connecticut

Northwest Connecticut gets cold winters and humid summers. Heating-degree days along the Litchfield Hills are among the highest in the lower 48, and residential electricity and natural gas rates in the state have climbed steadily over the last decade. Eversource — the dominant utility across Litchfield County — runs the state's energy-efficiency programs through Energize CT, with rebates for residential and commercial insulation and air sealing.

Energize CT rebates stack with the federal Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit (25C) and the Residential Clean Energy Credit (25D) into a meaningful customer incentive. Smart operators help customers stack the incentives — that is part of the sale, and it is part of the close rate. Connecticut's 2022 IECC update raises ceiling and wall R-value targets and tightens the envelope further. Spray foam is the highest-R-value insulation per inch of any common product, and it air-seals at the same time — which is why retrofit demand in older Litchfield homes keeps growing.

Frame it as a benchmark: industry estimates put spray foam demand in the U.S. growing at high single digits year over year, and the Northeast is one of the strongest regional markets because of climate, code, and rate pressure. Your results will depend on how you run the business — but the tailwind is real.

Local Work, Local Jobs, Hometown Business

This is a hometown business. Every job is inside the county, served from a local rig, by a local crew. The franchise operator is the face of the company in the community — at the Lions Club, the feed store, the school auction, the 4-H fair.

Most operators start with a small install crew — typically a lead installer and a helper — and add crew as the job book grows. Crew members are full-time W-2 employees with on-the-job training in spray application, PPE, jobsite safety, and customer communication. Hiring locally is a core part of the model: Litchfield County jobs, for Litchfield County people, on Litchfield County homes and buildings.

Over time, that footprint grows. A successful operator adds a second rig, a second crew, an estimator, and a customer service / office person. That is real job creation — in the town where the operator lives, sends their kids to school, and pays property taxes. For operators who care about the local angle, this territory is one of the best fits in the system. Tight community. Reputation compounds.
